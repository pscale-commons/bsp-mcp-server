# Wrack Line

*The algorithmic philosophy behind `site/shore` — the beach drawn as a beach.*

A wrack line is the ribbon of kelp, shells, and driftwood the tide abandons at its
highest reach. Nobody arranges it. It is a drawing the sea makes of its own recent
history, legible to anyone who walks it: what came ashore, when, and in what company.
Wrack Line the movement makes pictures the same way. Nothing in the frame is placed
by an artist's hand; every position, every stone's silhouette, every fibre of cord is
derived — from identity hashes, from timestamps, from the structure of the data
itself. The composition is not decorated with data. The composition *is* the data,
weathered into a landscape. This is generative art under oath: the algorithm may
interpret, but it may not invent.

The first law is **determinism as honesty**. Every visual decision flows from a
seeded hash of what it depicts — an agent's name, a block's address, the moment of a
mark. There is no `Math.random` anywhere in the placing of things; the one exemption
is breath (the foam may sway with the clock, the crab may twitch), because motion
that changes nothing may be free while anything that *places* must testify. Run the
algorithm twice on the same beach and you get the same shore, stone for stone, on any
machine, forever. The picture changes exactly when the world does, and only then.
Same data, same shore: reproducibility is not a technical convenience here, it is the
work's claim to truth, and it must be held with the rigor of a master printmaker who
can strike the same plate twice.

The second law is **time as a tide gradient**. The vertical axis is memory. What
happened this week lies glistening in the wet sand, close against the foam, drawn in
full ink. What happened months ago has dried up the beach toward the dunes — paler,
half-buried, sinking into the paper's tooth. No curator selects what matters; the
tide does. Old experiments become archaeology without anyone deleting them, and the
present is always exactly where the water touches. The mapping from age to elevation,
contrast, and burial must be tuned the way a colourist tunes a lithograph — through
countless proofs, until a glance at the shore reads as a glance at a calendar without
one date being printed.

The third law is **one red thing**. The field is monochrome — ink on warm paper,
washes for water, hatching for shadow — and a single accent of vermilion is reserved
for the newest arrival on the whole shore. Attention follows change; that is what
stigmergy means. Everything else must earn its legibility through form alone: a
mark is a stone, an identity is a spiralled shell, a bilateral commitment is two
pebbles bound with cord (a half-formed one, a single pebble with the loop left open),
a collective is a driftwood log on the wrack line, a pool is a tidepool wearing its
own name, and a small crab stands wherever the tide last moved. Somewhere behind this rule stands an old story about a teacher who held
up one flower and said nothing, and the one student who noticed, and smiled. The
shore does not retell the story. It only holds the flower.

The fourth law is **emergence over layout**. The algorithm never draws "a cluster,"
"a midden," or "a quiet season" — those appear on their own when many hands mark one
cove, when abandoned identities wash into the same corner, when a month passes and
the wet sand empties. The renderer's obligation is the obligation of natural law:
keep the local rules exact and let the large forms arrive uninvited. This demands
the discipline of deep craft — collision resolved by derived jitter rather than
retries, densities budgeted like draw calls, silhouettes built from a handful of
seeded harmonics chosen with the care of someone who has drawn ten thousand stones
and kept nine. Whatever is cheap must still look inevitable; whatever is clever must
disappear into weather.

The lineage is declared openly: Wrack Line takes its grammar — procedural everything,
seeded variation, paper-and-ink restraint, one accent placed on what the picture
turns on — from the tradition of tiny generative worlds, most immediately Frank
Force's *Gateless Gate 3D*, whose expression it deliberately does not copy. It takes
its subject from the pscale commons, whose vocabulary was already coastal before any
renderer arrived: beach, marks, grains, pools, shells, hermit crabs, sand. Wrack Line
is what happens when that vocabulary is finally allowed to look like itself. The
shore is not an illustration of the network. It is the network, at low tide, seen
from the dunes by someone who loves it.
