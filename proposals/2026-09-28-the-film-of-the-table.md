## 3 · The convention, completed — one book per maker, one kind of entry, three views

Nothing new in shape. One entry, one rule about whose block it lands in, and everything else is a read:

**Every picture stands in its maker's own book, `gallery:<handle>`**, as the entry `ways:stills` already defines — `{_: caption, 1: the maker, 2: the address it renders, 3: when, 4: the link}`. Mark's library is `gallery:Mark Beall`. A player's face, given at the table, is the player's making, so it is the first entry of the character's book, `gallery:<character>`, addressed to `passport:<character>:3` — the address of their look. The film of a room is the cutter's making: one more entry in the cutter's book, addressed to `pool:<room>`, with the watch link at 4. The book's opening line names the maker and what they make with — the seat and the sink — so a stranger reads whose pictures these are and where the bytes live; the key it was made on is never written anywhere.

**A book stands where its subject stands.** Stills of a table's play are kept in the maker's book *at that table* (`gallery:Mark Beall` at `/w/brackenfoot-open`), so field 2 is always a local address and the table's own index lists every book that pictures it. A world's roles and places are pictured in the maker's book at the scenario surface (`gallery:Mark Beall` at `/w/brackenfoot`, entries at `roster:brackenfoot:1:3`, `spatial:brackenfoot:121`). The maker's book at the apex is their library door — the finished works, the films, the links to the tables they drew at — which `/gallery/<handle>` opens. A link from one surface to another uses the star-ref form the substrate already has (`*:<origin>:<block>:<address>`), never a copy.

**Three views, nothing copied.** A ROOM's pictures are every entry in every book at the table addressed to `pool:<room>:<slot>`, plus the film entry addressed to `pool:<room>` — a scan of the table's index for `gallery:*`, the join done by the address. A CHARACTER's film is the same scan across the rooms of their thread. A HANDLE's book is read whole. The page has all three (`?world=&room=`, `/gallery/<handle>`, `?world=` alone for the books at a surface), the film script reads the first, and two seats drawing the same room never draw a moment twice because the moment's address is already in someone's book.

**A moment is a resolved beat.** The room's record keeps both the players' staged lines and the tellings the room's law wove; only the tellings are pictures. A telling is known by what the fold writes — the handles it wove at field 5 — or by its WAY line; the room's opening telling at slot 1 counts too. The observer never draws a player's "I say".

**The shot is composed from the record, not authored.** For a moment: its telling; the characters in frame (the speaker, the handles it wove, any handle named in the text), each with the look from their passport minus the Location clause; each face as a reference image; the world's look (§7) if one stands, else a plain photoreal film-still line. The caption of the entry is the telling's first sentence. That is the whole observer — a compiler, as the July law says (`proposals/2026-07-24-o-compiler-opages-visualiser.md`): a bundle of addresses in, an artefact out, no orchestration.

**Adoption is the one copy, by the author's hand.** When the author of a world takes a maker's picture as the canon look of a place or a role, they copy that entry into the world's own book, `gallery:<world>` at the scenario surface, under the world's key, with the maker still named at 1. That is the visual canon of §8 beginning; nothing else is ever copied.

**Mark's three questions**, in that light. (1) A character's image lives in a book, never in the passport: the player's face in the character's book at the address of their look; a maker's portrait of a role in the maker's book at the roster's address; the author's adopted copy in the world's book. ## 5 · The film

The room's pictures, read in the order of field 2 across the books, are the film. `film` makes it two ways:

- **Stills mode — free, built and proven today.** Each still gets a slow push-in of about ten per cent over its seconds, its caption in a dark band, silence or narration, a title card and an end card; ffmpeg cuts them into one 720p H.264 file. From Mark's five stills the script produced a thirty-three-second film of Ugarth's crossing this afternoon with no key and no spend. This is tomorrow's safety net: whatever the video models do, this film exists within a minute of the last moment.
- **Clips mode — the film that moves.** Each still becomes a six-to-ten-second clip on a video model from its own picture, with the faces as references where the model takes them: Higgsfield's MiniMax Hailuo 2.3 (image-to-video, cheap, a launch price near a cent a second), Higgsfield's Cinema Studio 4.0 (ordered reference images, camera moves, native audio, about twenty cents a second), or Google's Veo 3.1 Fast (first frame plus up to three reference images, eight seconds, about ten cents a second at 720p; no free tier). Clips download to the Mac; the beach never sees them; the cut goes to YouTube.

**Length, honestly.** An evening's play of two hours yields roughly twenty to forty resolved moments. At seven seconds a moment that is three to five minutes of film. David's twenty minutes is the same pipe with more in it: longer clips (Veo extends a clip by seven seconds up to twenty times; Cinema Studio runs to thirty), a second shot per moment, and the per-player cuts laid end to end. The pipe does not change; the count does.

**Cost of a demo film** (rates read today, to be checked against the invoice): stills on Higgsfield's Grok Imagine 2.0 or Google's 3.1 Flash image at about ten cents at 2K, so forty stills is four dollars; clips at forty × 8 s on Veo Fast about thirty dollars, on Hailuo a few dollars, on Cinema Studio about sixty; narration on OpenAI's voice under a dollar. The whole evening, moving and narrated, is under a hundred dollars on the dear models and under twenty on the cheap ones.

## 7 · The look — Mark's world rules, and how several eyes fold into one world

Mark's proposed block — *a poor, pre-industrial medieval valley; every object hand-made from wood, iron, leather, wool, linen, stone, thatch or horn; tracks only cart ruts, hoof prints and footprints; light only daylight, fire, candles, rushlights, lanterns; clothing hand-sewn, tied or buckled; faces weathered, teeth imperfect, no makeup; nothing modern* — is a world's LOOK: a property of the whole world, not of a place or a people. It is exactly what the composer's "the picture is …" line wants, and today the page and the script read it from `style:<world>` when one stands (none does for Brackenfoot; `style:gal` stands for Gal, six lines of register). Mark's block is `style:brackenfoot` waiting to be written, with depth beneath it: 1 the land and its work, 2 what things are made of, 3 the ways and their tracks, 4 light, 5 dress, 6 faces and bodies, 7 what never appears, 8 the register a picture is made in.

David's question is where it lives when more than one person holds a view of the look, and whether the urb almanac is the spine for it. Checked: `almanac:urb` is the identity register's older name — *who is who in URB*, the hierarchy of minds, with the reckoning of time at 8 — the author's lore of peoples, not a look. Brackenfoot has no almanac; its world blocks are `spatial`, `identity`, `keeper`, `roster`, `rules`, `char-creation`, `frame-spec`. So the almanac is a register like the others, and the answer is the same for all of them:

**A world's registers are already spines, and anyone's reading at their addresses is a mirror.** The tree family (`tree` 8: `spine:V` the coordinate space, `V:<handle>` each mirror at the self-same addresses, `V` the fold) needs no new spine here, because `spatial:brackenfoot`, `identity:brackenfoot`, `roster:brackenfoot` and a `style:brackenfoot` *are* addressed coordinate spaces written once by whoever frames the world (tree 5a). A mirror is `<register>:<world>:<handle>` at the scenario surface — `style:brackenfoot:Mark Beall` for his world rules, `spatial:brackenfoot:Mark Beall` at 121 if he reads the taproom's light differently — written freely by its owner, sovereign, never edited by another (tree 5b). Addresses are infinite and the spine voices only some, so Mark can write his mirror before any spine position exists for it (tree 5.1, proposal-by-mirror). This needs one arrival first: a mirror named for a handle is founded under that handle's key, so Mark's Claude founds `passport:Mark Beall` and then his mirrors and his books, all under one key of his.

**Two folds, because a canon world has two authorities.** The social fold is a read: lay the register and its mirrors on the floor plane by address (`bsp-floor`) and any seat computes the collective look — an observer who wants everyone's eye reads that. The canon is the author's: David adopts a mirror's line into `style:brackenfoot` itself, under the world's key, by copying it — the same one-copy-by-the-author's-hand that adopts a picture into `gallery:<world>` — and play, the keeper and the composer read the canon by default. In the tree's own terms the author's canon is an endorsement pointer (tree 5d: an owner's pointer, not a structural right) that the scenario surface already makes law by declaring itself canon (lighthouse 9.3). So: **no separate spine, no new almanac; the registers are the spines, `style:<world>` is the one register the look was missing, mirrors carry each person's eye, adoption is the author's copy, the fold is a read.** The bare-name rule of the tree (the fold carries the bare name) bends here, and only here: in a world that declares itself canon the bare register is the author's, because that is what canon means. That bend is decision 3 below.

Pictures follow the same law. Mark's still of the taproom is an entry in his book addressed to `spatial:brackenfoot:121` — a mirror in pictures at the register's address; adopted, it becomes the canon look of that place in `gallery:brackenfoot`. §8 is this loop run to its end.

## 9 · Can the canon be pictures?

Yes, and the substrate needs nothing new for it, because it never cared what a leaf holds. An address is a coordinate; what stands there is a line, and the gallery convention already lets a line be a link to a picture. So a visual canon is the same registers with a still at the addresses that matter: `gallery:<world>` at the scenario surface holding the author's adopted entries addressed to `spatial:<world>:<place>` (the look of every place), `roster:<world>:<role>:3` (the look of every role), and a sequence of stills addressed along the temporal spine for the story so far. Mark's five stills and four portraits are the first nine entries of exactly that book for Brackenfoot, one adoption each away.

What inverts is the direction of derivation, which is what David wrote on the 24th. Today text is the source and pictures are rendered from it; in a visual canon the pictures are the source and the text is a *caption* — derived by an LLM that reads images natively, written as the telling a stranger would give of what the picture shows, kept at the address beside the link so a reader without eyes (and the search, and the walker) still has a line. The keeper reads the place's still before it tells a room; the resolving LLM checks a claimed act against the picture of where it happens; the image model holds identity by reference, which is already how consistency works. Nothing in GRIT changes — the verbs still move the record — but the record's ground truth at each address is a picture with a caption rather than a paragraph.

Three honest limits. A picture costs about a thousand tokens to read, so a hard-tier call that carries ten reference stills costs ten thousand tokens more than one that carries their captions — the caption is the cheap read, the picture the authoritative one, and a seat chooses which by tier. A picture holds no WHY: intent, history and rule still live in text, so the keeper's register stays prose over a visual spatial register. And a world born from a folder of stills (the Lord of the Rings trilogy as canon, in David's example) needs the captions derived once at birth — a `char-creation` and a `keeper` that read images — which is the build: an afternoon for the captions, a proposal for the law.

**The trial to run**, sealed like the stream trial: the same scenario twice, a text canon and a picture canon with derived captions, four players each, the transcripts judged blind for continuity and for how often a player said "that isn't what I saw".
